The finding

Green-blue infrastructure (GBI) and nature-based solutions (NbS) are already an operational vocabulary in transboundary water governance, not a rhetorical garnish. Where they appear, they are mostly about flood risk and water retention, framed as a deliberate move from grey (engineered) to green (natural) measures, and the densest, most concrete language comes from joint basin commissions rather than single governments. The reason is structural. Upstream retention and restoration reduce downstream flood and pollution risk, and upstream and downstream sit in different countries, so GBI is a coordination problem by construction. One line from the corpus makes the governance case directly.

Nature-based solutions to water allocation infrastructure rarely have negative transboundary impacts, while they simultaneously help to meet environmental requirements. Handbook on Water Allocation in a Transboundary Context, UNECE (2021)

That reframes NbS as a positive-sum measure that sidesteps the zero-sum logic of water allocation, which makes it a durable, low-conflict agenda for riparian states to cooperate on.

The transboundary link

The documents that treat GBI best are written by joint basin bodies, and they set shared, quantified restoration targets across sovereign borders.

Danube · 11 countries

The ICPDR holds a basin-wide vision that "floodplains/wetlands all over the DRBD are re-connected and restored," noting 80% are currently disconnected.

Rhine · international district

Quantified joint targets: over 5,650 river-km of nature restoration and over 1,230 km² of floodplain reactivation by 2027, allocated across riparian nations.

Dniester · Moldova & Ukraine

Jointly created the Lower Dniester National Park, more than 600 km² on both banks, using ecosystem-based flood control.

SADC · Southern Africa

A regional strategy asks to build member-state capacity on NbS "for more resilient management of transboundary river basins and aquifers."

The influence runs both ways. GBI needs transboundary governance to coordinate and share costs and benefits; and basin organisations are adopting GBI as a cheaper, cooperative alternative to grey infrastructure. Costing upstream retention and restoration as a cross-border service is a natural next step for joint financing and benefit-sharing between states.

Takeaways for the talk

  1. GBI / NbS is now operational language in transboundary water governance, not decoration. 397 statements, 86 official documents, a third already at binding or operational depth.
  2. It is fundamentally a coordination story. Upstream retention is downstream flood and pollution defence, and the strongest evidence is joint basin commissions setting shared, quantified targets across borders.
  3. The dominant move is grey-to-green plus flood-driven retention: stepping back from concrete-first defence toward floodplains, wetlands and forests that deliver several benefits at once.
  4. NbS is the low-conflict entry point for cooperation. It rarely creates negative transboundary impacts, which makes it a natural first axis for a Transboundary Water Circularity Index.
